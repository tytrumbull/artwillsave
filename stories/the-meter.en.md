Every morning at 8:40, Walter put two quarters into the parking meter outside the laundromat. He did not own a car.

The car belonged to someone he had never met. A green hatchback, dented on the passenger side, with a parking pass from the hospital hanging off the mirror. It arrived at 8:30 and left around six, and for the first week Walter watched the meter run out at 10:30 and the ticket go on the windshield at 10:45, regular as a church bell.

So he started feeding it.

His daughter, when he told her on the phone, said this was exactly the kind of thing that worried her. "You're on a pension, Dad."

"It's fifty cents."

"It's fifty cents three times a day. That's a lot of fifty cents."

He did the math after he hung up. She was right. It came to about thirty dollars a month, which was what he used to spend on cigarettes before he quit, so he decided it was really the cigarettes paying for it, and that settled it.

In March the car stopped coming.

Walter fed the empty meter for three days anyway, out of something like habit and something like superstition. On the fourth day there was an envelope taped to the post, with his name on it. Nobody on this street knew his name, he would have said, but apparently somebody did.

Inside was a roll of quarters and a note.

*She's home now. She asked me every night if the meter man had come. Thank you for the parking. Thank you for the rest.*

He kept the note. He spent the quarters, one pair at a time, on whoever parked there next.
